\begin{abstract}
Learned controllers are commonly commissioned behind runtime assurance: a
trusted shield or safety filter holds action authority while the policy
adapts, so every training transition satisfies the safety certificate.
Deployments then retire that authority and release the raw policy after a
finite check.  We show that the check does not transfer across this
retirement.  Evidence collected under one authority is reused to authorize a
different subject over a longer horizon, a flaw we call evidence reuse across
an authority transition.

The gap does not require an adversary.  In a controlled Safe-Control-Gym
study, snapshots that passed a five-step release check failed on 2 of 120
snapshot--state pairs once released raw, whereas the same prediction retained
at runtime failed on none; a one-step filter did not suffice.  An upstream
attacker amplifies the gap rather than creating it: an adversary holding only
bounded write access to reward records raised raw-release failures to 27 of
120 with no training-time violation, and raised raw-policy violations at
retirement by factors of 3.6 and 1.6 on a
published third-party shield-retirement lifecycle.  Retained predictive
authority switched before all 27 failures.  Trusted recomputation closes this
channel; it does not close the contract gap the channel exploits.
\end{abstract}
