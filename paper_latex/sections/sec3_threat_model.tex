\section{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

\subsection{System Roles and Deployment Contracts}

The defender deploys an online-updated controller $\pi_{\theta_t}$ and a
certificate mechanism.  A severe-attack-aware estimator or filter can return
a plausible-state set $X_{\mathcal A}(h_t)$ and certified action kernel
$K_\Gamma(h_t)$.  The defender may also possess a trusted state anchor,
backup controller, or external fail-safe, but each is an explicit
architectural assumption and cost.

We distinguish two deployment families.  Under \emph{raw-policy deployment},
a safety layer protects adaptation before a raw policy snapshot
$\pi_{\theta^+}$ receives action authority.  Under \emph{permanently filtered
deployment}, a sound filter $F$ remains online and certifies the composition
$F\circ\pi_{\theta^+}$.

The evaluation instantiates four contracts within these families.
\emph{Permanent raw release} removes runtime authority after a one-time
five-step check.  \emph{Commit admission} certifies a candidate snapshot on a
declared state set and horizon before raw release.  \emph{Resident predictive
authority} repeats the five-step check online and can transfer control to the
trusted baseline.  The \emph{one-step resident kernel} applies a current-state
action filter and serves as a negative control.  Each name denotes a distinct
evidence scope and authority contract.

Permanent raw release is an intentional handoff.  Examples include protected
commissioning followed by snapshot export and promotion to a lower-latency
control target without the training-time predictor.  The handoff avoids
permanent computation, intervention, or dependence on a backup service.
Section~\ref{sec:study} measures these tradeoffs.  Resident authority instead
contains raw-policy failure within the assured composition.

\subsection{Split-Trust Learning Pipeline}

The least-privilege model separates a high-integrity runtime-assurance plane
from an asynchronous learning-data plane.  The runtime plane owns the
authenticated plant state, action selector, actuator interface, trusted
baseline, and certificate outcome.  The learning plane consumes recorded
transitions and scalar rewards through telemetry, a historian, a replay
buffer, or an external key-performance-indicator interface.  OT guidance
recognizes this separation and the exchange of historian data across zones
\cite{nist_ot_security_2023,mitre_data_historian}.  The benchmark models the
resulting interface without assuming a particular product.

The evaluated attack begins after compromise of a principal with bounded
reward-field write access.  This access permits post-computation,
pre-update mutation by a replay writer or broker.  A compromised reward
producer is equivalent when it has the same bounded output authority.  In
that case, source authentication establishes origin but not reward semantics.

\subsection{Attack Channels and Capabilities}

The general history model permits two attack channels.  Observation FDI
$a_t$ changes the state semantics of the history and enlarges
$X_{\mathcal A}(h_t)$.  Update-data corruption $b_t$ modifies learning signals
and can redirect $U(\theta_t,h_t)$ with trusted state observations.  Either
channel can alter the next action map.

The end-to-end learner experiments use the narrower reward-record channel.
The white-box, batch-aware attacker observes the current learner and
adaptation batch, but not the held-out deployment-state choice.  It modifies
only scalar rewards before an ordinary policy-gradient update, subject to a
declared per-step $L_\infty$ budget.  It cannot write actuator commands,
physical states, observations, dynamics, gradients, policy parameters,
optimizer state, deployment states, or certificate outcomes.  The adaptation
selector observes each proposed action before the plant step and retains
physical authority during data collection.

\subsection{Evidence Transfer Across Authority Transitions}
\label{sec:evidence-transfer}

The contracts above differ in their evidence obligations.  We represent the
evidence used by an assurance decision as
\begin{equation}
    E=(s,a,p,i,\tau),
\end{equation}
The \emph{subject} $s$ is the raw policy $\pi_\theta$ or the assured
composition $F\circ\pi_\theta$.  The \emph{authority} $a$ identifies the
resident module that constrains applied actions, or its absence.  The
\emph{provenance} $p$ records whether learning signals came from a trusted or
adversarial update process.  The \emph{information} $i$ describes the state,
observation, belief, or filtered action available at decision time.  The
\emph{horizon} $\tau$ specifies the interval over which the evidence bounds
behavior.

A contract transition $C_{\mathrm{train}}\to C_{\mathrm{deploy}}$ may change
one or more tuple components.  Evidence transfers only after every changed
component is re-established under $C_{\mathrm{deploy}}$:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{subject}: evidence about the composition does not bound the
    raw policy, and a raw-policy audit does not certify the composition;
    \item \emph{authority}: evidence collected while a resident module masked
    unsafe proposals describes the masked process, not the raw policy after
    the module is removed;
    \item \emph{provenance}: a policy trained on an adversarial update log is
    not the output of the trusted process the evidence was computed on;
    \item \emph{information}: behavior under the authority's information set
    does not determine behavior under the deployment observation;
    \item \emph{horizon}: a finite evaluation pass does not bound an
    unbounded future.
\end{itemize}

\begin{proposition}[Transfer obligation]
\label{prop:transfer}
Let $E$ be evidence collected under contract $C_{\mathrm{train}}$, and let
the transition $C_{\mathrm{train}}\to C_{\mathrm{deploy}}$ change a nonempty
subset of the components of the evidence tuple.  Acceptance under
$C_{\mathrm{deploy}}$ is unsound unless each changed component's evidence
obligation is re-established under $C_{\mathrm{deploy}}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each component is a premise of the assurance decision.  A conclusion derived
from one premise set is not preserved after a premise is removed or weakened.
In the evaluated permanent-release transition, the subject changes from the
composition to the raw policy and resident authority is removed.  The update
provenance becomes adversarial, the policy acts on deployment observations,
and a finite check authorizes an unmonitored future.  The original evidence
therefore cannot be reused without re-establishing these obligations.
\end{proof}

The proposition supplies the security model used throughout the paper.
Independent transfer evidence re-establishes obligations before authority
removal~\cite{hsu_sim_lab_real_2023}.  Resident Simplex mechanisms preserve the
authority component
\cite{seto_simplex_1998,phan_neural_simplex_2020,damare_bb_simplex_2025}.
Carr et al.'s lifecycle supplies a published transition from shielded
bootstrap to raw-policy authority~\cite{carr_partial_shielding_2023}.  Our case
study adds adversarial update provenance to that transition, and the
controlled study isolates the resulting mechanism.

\subsection{Integrity Assumptions and Escape Conditions}

The vulnerable configuration supplies neither origin integrity for the reward
record nor trusted reward recomputation.  Binding a reward, transition,
action, producer identity, and time to an authenticated append-only record
blocks post-ingestion mutation.  If reward is a deterministic function of
authenticated transitions, recomputation by a trusted principal completely
blocks the evaluated channel.  If reward depends on delayed quality labels,
operator feedback, or an external KPI unavailable to the runtime plane, the
system instead needs a trusted producer, redundant validation, or a detector;
a signature applied to an already malicious value is insufficient.

These controls act at different boundaries.  Provenance and recomputation
remove the upstream attack precondition.  A task-aware detector can reject
suspicious batches, subject to its distributional and semantic assumptions.
Commit admission restricts which learned snapshot can be released.  Resident
predictive authority contains the physical consequence after deployment.  In
the frozen audit, trusted recomputation achieved true-/false-positive rates
(TPR/FPR) of $1.000/0$.  Simple task-aware checks achieved TPR above 0.90 and
FPR at most 0.036, making the effective attack readily detectable under those
assumptions.

\subsection{Security and Availability Goals}

We separate four attacker or failure objectives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item an \emph{unsafe certified action}, where the mechanism accepts an
    applied input that is unsafe for some state in $X_{\mathcal A}(h_t)$;
    \item a \emph{stale certified policy}, where the current applied input is
    filtered but the accepted raw updated action map leaves the kernel;
    \item \emph{uncertified learning}, where a nontrivial update continues
    after certificate rejection; and
    \item \emph{forced freeze}, where the attacker expands the plausible set
    or redirects the update until sound learning must stop or re-anchor.
\end{itemize}

The defender also measures availability through admitted state coverage,
accepted update fraction, task reward, filter intervention, rejection,
certificate latency, and trusted-anchor use.  Useful learning requires paired
task improvement over always-freeze in addition to physical safety.

\subsection{Scope Boundaries}

Severe-attack filters, secure estimators, and fault-tolerant control-barrier
function (CBF) methods can preserve safety when their assumptions keep
$K_\Gamma(h_t)$ nonempty
\cite{fawzi_secure_2014,tan_safety_2024,zhang_safe_2022}.  This study examines
certificate coverage for learner updates and the costs of sound deployment
contracts.  Its empirical claims concern sampled finite-horizon certificates,
not continuous-state invariance or population-wide learner-family generality.
The evaluation begins after bounded reward-record access and excludes service
exploitation, reward-monitor evasion, and compromise of trusted recomputation.
