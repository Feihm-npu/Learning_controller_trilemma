\section{Introduction}

Runtime assurance allows a controller to adapt while a Simplex decision
module, runtime shield, or model-predictive safety filter retains action
authority.  The runtime-assurance mechanism replaces unsafe policy proposals with
actions from an integrity-protected fallback controller.  In our reported
adaptation runs, every executed transition satisfied the measured safety
constraints~\cite{seto_simplex_1998,alshiekh_shielding_2018,
wabersich_linear_mpsc_2018}.  Some learning workflows nevertheless phase out a
shield to reduce conservatism or permanent runtime cost, whereas others
establish separate transfer evidence before deployment
\cite{carr_partial_shielding_2023,hsu_sim_lab_real_2023}.  A release workflow
may also test the raw policy over a bounded prediction or rollout horizon.
Protection evidence and a bounded raw-policy test support different claims:
the former covers the executed composition, and the latter covers the tested
policy only over its declared horizon.

We audit two controller handoffs.  The first moves from shielded adaptation to
raw-policy execution.  In the controlled study, a five-step raw-policy test
re-establishes evidence for the released subject over five steps, but not for
the remaining 115 steps of a 120-step deployment.  The clean comparison
therefore measures horizon extrapolation after release rather than treating
safe shielded execution as evidence that the raw policy is intrinsically safe.
The second handoff occurs when a raw-policy lookahead switcher transfers control
to a fallback controller.  The trigger predicts the outgoing policy, whereas
safe transfer depends on the fallback's response from the handoff state.  We
use \emph{evidence gaps across controller handoffs} for cases in which the
evidence supplied to a decision does not cover the subject, authority,
provenance, information, or horizon required by the post-handoff contract.
Reward-record corruption stresses these gaps by changing the provenance of the
learning input.

A clean cohort fixes the ordering without an attacker.  Twenty independently
trained controllers supplied 480 snapshot--state pairs accepted by the clean
five-step raw-policy test.  Raw execution over 120 steps failed on 10 pairs
distributed across five training runs, whereas the raw-policy lookahead
switcher failed on none.  All 10 paired discordances favoured the switcher
(two-sided exact
$p=0.00195$), and the run-level failure frequency was 5/20 (Wilson 95\% CI
$[11.2\%,46.9\%]$).  State pairs within a trained controller are repeated
measurements, so the run count, not 480, supplies training-level replication.
No attacker is involved in this separation.

An upstream attacker amplifies the observed failures rather than creating the
underlying evidence boundaries.  While the shield masks unsafe proposals,
corrupted update data steers the learner without a physical violation during
the reported adaptation runs.  Bounded reward-record edits raised a separate
120-pair raw-execution count from 2 to 27.  A sign/envelope-constrained attacker
remained effective as the protected bootstrap grew.  At 48 update batches it
caused 46/116 raw-execution failures and 34/116 failures under the lookahead
switcher.  Paired outcomes were 27 failures under both contracts, 19 only under
raw execution, seven only under the switcher, and 63 under neither.  All 34
switcher failures followed transfer to the integrity-protected LQR fallback,
and violation occurred after a median of one additional step.  The seven
switcher-only failures show that detecting risk in the raw policy did not
establish safe recovery by the receiving controller.  We call the attack
\emph{shield-retirement poisoning}.

The attack applies when the runtime plane cannot reconstruct reward semantics
from authenticated transitions (Section~\ref{sec:threat}).  Trusted
recomputation removes the channel when such reconstruction is available.  It
does not extend a finite release test beyond its horizon or establish the
safety of a subsequent fallback handoff.

Operational technology (OT) guidance separates runtime-control assets from the
data-management services and historians that exchange information across
zones~\cite{nist_ot_security_2023,mitre_data_historian}, so we make the
attacker deliberately narrow in authority and start the threat model after
compromise of a reward-ingestion principal in that learning-data plane.  This
is an explicit split-trust precondition: the attacker may modify bounded
scalar reward records consumed by a policy-gradient updater, and reads the
learner state those edits are computed against.  Everything on the
authenticated control path stays outside its write authority
(Table~\ref{tab:trust-boundary}, Fig.~\ref{fig:lifecycle-placeholder}).

Prior work establishes the relevant safety obligations.  Shielded learning
does not make the learned policy intrinsically safe, and online shielding work
therefore recommends retaining protection during execution
\cite{konighofer_online_shielding_2022}.  Simplex and model-predictive safety
certification use recoverable regions, safe target sets, or switching
conditions to justify transfer to a fallback controller
\cite{seto_simplex_1998,phan_neural_simplex_2020,
wabersich_linear_mpsc_2018,damare_bb_simplex_2025}.  Carr et al. study gradual
and sudden shield retirement, while Hsu et al. establish independent transfer
evidence before deployment
\cite{carr_partial_shielding_2023,hsu_sim_lab_real_2023}.  We do not claim these
obligations as new.  We test whether the bounded evidence supplied by the
evaluated release and warning rules satisfies them, and how reward-record
poisoning changes the resulting failures.

\subsection{Evidence and Contributions}

The evidence gaps and the channel that stresses them are separate objects.  The
clean Safe-Control-Gym cohort estimates how often five-step evidence failed to
extend to 120-step raw execution across independent training runs.  A horizon
sweep measures warning time from the repeated raw-policy check.  The duration
sweep then evaluates the distinct transfer to the LQR fallback.  Carr et al.'s
published workflow provides an independent shield-retirement lifecycle
\cite{carr_partial_shielding_2023}.  Adding bounded reward-record poisoning only
during its protected bootstrap increased raw-policy violations at retirement
by factors of 3.6 and 1.6 in two of three provenance-complete paired REINFORCE
training runs.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We provide a dependency-aware audit of handoff evidence over subject,
    authority, provenance, information, and horizon.  A changed field prompts
    reassessment; evidence must be replaced only when the post-handoff contract
    no longer supplies a premise on which that evidence depends.
    \item We quantify clean horizon extrapolation over 20 independently trained
    controllers, comparing one-time five-step acceptance with 120-step raw
    execution and a repeated raw-policy lookahead switcher.  A locked horizon
    sweep measures warning time and admission loss.
    \item We instantiate bounded reward-record writes in the controlled study
    and in a third-party shield-retirement lifecycle.  In the
    sign/envelope-constrained duration sweep, the lookahead switcher prevented
    19 raw-execution failures but had seven switcher-only failures.  The result
    isolates fallback recovery from the warning supplied about the raw policy.
    \item We separate controls by contract.  Trusted recomputation closes the
    upstream reward channel; commit admission restricts raw-policy release;
    a lookahead switcher requires independently supported fallback recovery;
    and adaptation should be deployed only when paired task benefit justifies
    its transition risks and costs.  Our evaluated benign shifts do not
    establish such a benefit.
\end{itemize}
